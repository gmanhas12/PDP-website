Belonging and access

Different ways to learn. Equal opportunity to belong.

Inclusivity means recognizing that students have different strengths, experiences and needs—and adapting the learning environment so everyone can participate meaningfully.

Universal design in practice

Plan for participation from the beginning

Our proposed classroom design and lesson planning aim to reduce barriers rather than expecting every student to learn in the same way.

Accessible spaces

Clear pathways, wide spacing, reachable materials and flexible work surfaces support students with different mobility needs.

Flexible communication

Written, visual, verbal, audio, translated and captioned information can help students access instructions and share their thinking.

Choice and flexibility

Students may demonstrate learning through making, drawing, writing, speaking, technology or role-play, depending on the task and their needs.

Examples of classroom supports

Supports are matched to the learner and the activity

Visual and language supports

Large, clear images, minimal visual clutter, labelled pictures, vocabulary banks, sentence starters, translated instructions and simple step-by-step guides.

Audio, tactile and communication supports

Audio or Braille formats, tactile materials, verbal guidance, captions, microphones, speech-to-text and opportunities to explain ideas orally.

Predictability and self-regulation

Clear routines, visual schedules, advance notice of transitions, quiet spaces, checklists and options for independent or supported work.

Physical access and materials

Accessible workspaces, reachable supplies, adapted or pre-prepared materials, extra time and school-provided resources when students may not have access at home.

Assessment and strengths

Keep the learning goals meaningful.

Accommodations change how students access learning or communicate what they know; they do not automatically lower the learning expectations. Teachers can assess understanding through observation, discussion, hands-on participation and different forms of expression.

For example, a student who finds writing difficult might explain a project verbally or use speech-to-text. A student with visual barriers might use tactile materials and verbal guidance. A student who benefits from predictable routines might receive a clear schedule and advance notice of transitions.

Partnership matters.
Teachers work with students, families and appropriate school support staff to understand strengths, identify barriers and choose useful strategies. Communication with families helps support continuity between school and home.
